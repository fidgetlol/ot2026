* suncor energy v county commissioners of boulder county // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/suncor-energy-inc-v-county-commissioners-of-boulder-county/
- Boulder Colorado sued various energy companies in state court for their contributions to climate change. Boulder argues that the energy companies knowingly contributed to climate change by working with fossil fuels. Those actions constitute public nuisance, private nuisance, trespass, unjust enrichment, and civil conspiracy. The energy companies moved to dismiss, arguing that Colorado exceeds its power in seeking releif from out of state conduct. The state court denied the motion and the Colorado Supreme Court affirmed. Now the energy companies appeal to SCOTUS.
** Is the suit precluded by federal law?
- By joining the union states accepted limits on sovereignty and application of the law. States cannot apply their local laws to conduct in beyond its borders. Air and water are shared natural resources and so must be governed by a shared national law.
- The Clean Air Act is the shared national law, a "comprehensive satutory scheme". The Clean Air Act does not authorize state common-law suits like this one. Before the Clean Air Act federal common law blocked this suit--the Clean Air Act can't be read to extend State law in this way.
- Interstate emmissions are inherently federal. Because the emissions that damaged Colorado happended globally, Colorado law cannot reach them.
- This suit intrudes on the federal government's control of foreign affairs. Having states go after the energy industry would get in the way of the federal government handing this global challenge.
- The EPA controls emissions standards under the Clean Air Act. This suit allows Colorado to impose emissions standards in violation of the Clean Air Act.

- State tort law reaches beyond state borders. When a product causes harm in one state the manufacturer isn't immune because it's in another state. Libel or internet fraud can originate anywhere in the country and still be heard in state court. The Consitution does not "silently forbid[] the practice". Congress can displace these state laws but they did not do so here.
- Federal common law didn't bar this suit. Federal common law was displaced by the Clean Air Act anyway. The Clean Air Act is concerned with regulation of emmitters, not with civil damages caused by them. This suit only asks for money, it's not seeking to impose any regulations or controls that could conflict with the Clean Air Act.
- Colorado law does not intrude on foreign affairs. The government failed to show a conflict with any "statute, self-executing treaty, or executive agreement." The generalized concerns expressed cannot alone displace state law.
- Congress can and is considering immunizing emitters from this type of suit. They have not done so.
** Does the court have jurisdiction?
- The dismissal below is not an Article 3 injury. Continued litigation is not an Article 3 injury. Petitioners must wait for a final judgement.

- The Colorado Supreme Court actually ruled on an order to show cause which is a self contained proceeding. That proceeding was terminated with the decision below. The termination of a self contained original proceeding in a state's court of last resort is appealable under 1257(a).
- The order to show cause was an interlocutory appeal. Colorado can name it an original proceeding but the label doesn't make it apealable. The nature of the proceeding determines whether it's a "final judgement in a self contained original proceeding" not a label.

* Johnson v United States Congress // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/johnson-v-united-states-congress/
- Floyd Johnson was in the US army from 1983 to 1985. In 2013 Johnson was sentenced to 40 years in Florida prison. While incarcerated Johnson was diagnosed with PTSD and applied for VA benefits. His benefits were reduced because the VA caps benefits for incarcerated vetrens. Johnson sued Congress pro se alledging violating the bill of attainder clause and the 5th amendment's equal protection clause. The District court dismissed the suit. On appeal to the 11th circuit Johnson was appointed council. The 11th circuit concluded that the suit is barred by sovereign immunity and that Johnson must follow the statutory review scheme. Johnson now appeals to SCOTUS.
** Does the Veterens' Judicial Review Act strip district courts of the jurisdiction to hear constitutional challenges to VA benefits?
- Johnson v Robinson established that courts can hear constitutional challenges to veterens benefits statutes even when the statute strips the court from jurisdiction to challenge benefits decisions.
- Petitioner argues that Congress kept the language at issue in Robinson and so kept the legal regieme it established. Veterens can challenge constitutionality of benefits statutes but cannot challenge benefits decision.
- Also the statutory review scheme directs Johnson to appeal to the Board of Veterens' Appeals which cannot review constitutional claims. This makes sense, the BVA is for challenging benefits decisions which isn't what Peitioner is trying to do.
- The government argues that Robinson is no longer relevant. The previous statutory regieme didn't include an apellete process so federal court was the only place. Now that the BVA exsists all challenges must go there.

* Anderson V. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/anderson-v-intel-corp-investment-policy-comm/
- In 2019 Anderson sued Intel's 401(k) investment managers. Anderson argues that the investment managers violated their fidutiary duties under ERISA by investing in hedge funds and private equity to benefit Intel. The district court dismissed the suit finding that Anderson failed to identify a "meaningful benchmark" to compare with Intel's funds and because he lacked sufficient factual basis in his self-dealing claims. The 9th circuit affirmed and now Anderson appeals to SCOTUS.
** Does Anderson's claim require a "meaningful benchmark" to establish underperformance?
- Anderson argues that Intel's fund invested in irregular assets and underperformed. These two facts satisfy ERISA and Rule 8. Nothing in the text of ERISA requires a "meaningful benchmark". The court created this additional requirement and it unlawfully heightens pleading requirements. The correct standard is whether the facts alledge allow a plausible inference of imprudence.
- The investment managers argue that you can't claim underperformance without a "meaningful benchmark". The "meaningful benchmark requirement" has been around for years and hasn't reduced sucessful ERISA sutis. Without a comparison to funds with similar goals the allegations are comparing growth funds to conservative funds looking to minimize volitility. Intel complied with their fidutiary duty by prudently persuing legitimate goals. Anderson's complaint lacks any benchmark and so fails to show the managers violated their duty.

* Department of the Air Force v. Prutehi Guahan // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/department-of-the-air-force-v-guahan/
- The Air Force uses a Tarague beach in northern Guam to dispose of unexploded ordnance. Under the RCRA the Air Force must receive a permit from the Guam EPA, and they did 1982. In May 2021 the Air force applied to renew their permit. Prutehi Guahan, a conservation nonprofit, sued The Air Force under the RCRA and NEPA. The district court dismissed the case, finding that Prutehi Guahan lacked standing before the Guam EPA made a final decision and, in any case, the Air Force wouldn't need to comply with NEPA to renew the permit. The 9th circuit reversed, finding that NEPA review could have led the Air Force to act differently (this would provide releif, so plaintiffs get standing), The Air Force's application is a "final agency action" under the APA, and the Air Force does need to comply with NEPA to renew the permit. The Air Force now appeals to SCOTUS.
** Was the Air Force's application for a permit a "final agency action"?
- The application is not the end of the decision making process. And the action is not "final" until the Guam EPA makes approves or denies it. Until that happens the Air Force is still deliberating with Guam and may ammend its application. Submitting an application doesn't make a "determination of legal rights or obligations". Guam, not the Air Force, is the one making that determination.
- The Air Force has decided to continue dispose of ordnance on Tarague beach. While the Guam EPA continues to process the application the Air Force continues to dispose of ordnance and Prutehi Guahan is harmed.
** Does the Government have to comply with NEPA when applying for a permit under the RCRA?
- The RCRA imposes specific requirements that displace NEPA's environmental review. The RCRA requires substantially the same review. The EPA has long held the position that RCRA applications are not subject to NEPA requirements--Congress has done nothing to reject that interpretation.
- There is nothing in the text of the RCRA that conflicts with or explicitly displaces NEPA. If the government can comply with both it must.

* Beaird v US // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/beaird-v-united-states/
- Bearid pleaded guilty to possession of a firearm as a convicted felon. The Sentencing Commision recommends a sentence enhancement when the firearm involved is "capable of accepting a large capacity magazine". The commentary section defines "large capacity magazine" as one that holds 15 or more rounds. Beaird's handgun's magazine held 17 rounds. 17 is more than 15. The judge sentenced him to 72 months in prison. Bearid appealed his sentence on various grounds. The 5th circuit shot him down. Now he appeals to SCOTUS. SCOTUS granted cert on only one question
** Does Stinson v United States still correctly state the rule of deference to the commentary in the sentencing guidelines?
- Stinson v US found that the Sentencing Guideline commentaries are authoritative unless they violate the constitution or a federal statute, or are inconsistent with or a plainly erroneous reading of the guideline.
- Bearid argues that the court should determine the best interpretation of the guidelines. Stinson was decided before the guidelines themselves were ruled advisory and before SCOTUS began rolling back deference to agency interpretation of the law. Overruning Stinson follows in line with those corrections.
- The government argues that the 5th circuit err'd in finding that the commentaries control even unambiguous guidelines. The Sentencing Commission is required to go through notice & comment for guidelines, the guidelines are then subject to congressional veto. The Sentncing Commission voluntarily goes through the same process for the commentaries. This gives these more weight than general rulemaking from administratie agencies. The "clearly erroneous" standard isn't as harsh as it sounds and correctly weighs the legitimacy of the commentaries.
- The Court Appointed an amicus to defend the 5th circuit opinion.
