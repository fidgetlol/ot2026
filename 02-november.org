* Maxwell v Dinis // https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/10/court-to-consider-federal-inmates-ability-to-use-habeas-to-accelerate-transfer-to-a-halfway-hous/
 - In 2007 Maxwell was part of an organized crime scheme to take over and loot a Texas mortgage company. He was the crime family's lawyer and received illegitimate consulting payments. In 2011 he was convicted of racketeering, various frauds, and firearms crimes. He was sentenced to 20 years in prison. Maxwell participated in First Step act programs which entitle him to time credit to reduce his sentence. The BoP accelerates placement to Residential Reentry Centers (RRC, halfway house) or home confinement based on these time credits. Maxwell argues that the BoP miscalculated his time credits and accelerated placement. Maxwell made a series of administrative appeals for accelerated transfer to home confinement. The appeals were denied. Maxwell then brought a habeas petition seeking priority transfer, claims for FSA time credits, preferential housing, additional phone minutes, expanded commissary, and transfer to RRC. The district court (E.D.Tx.) dismissed finding that the BoP has discretion in granting request for transfer to home confinement, and finding that the Maxwell failed to exhaust administrative remedies for the rest of his claims. Maxwell appealed the failure to exhaust for the FSA time credits. The 5th circuit affirmed the dismissal, finding that Habeas isn't the proper vehicle for challenges to conditions of confinement. Now Maxwell appeals to SCOTUS.
** Can an FSA claim seeking accelerated transfer to RRC or home confinement be brought under Habeas?
- Scalia wrote that habeas relief "includes ordering a quantum change in the level of custody". Ginsburg later quoted that in a majority (in tortured prose).
- 2255(e) requires habeas petitions to "test the legality of (petitioner's) detention". Maxwell's detention is in a prison and he claims the BoP violates the law by denying him transfer to home confinement.
- Maxwell cannot bring this case under 1983 as it is inapplicable against federal agents. And he cannot bring it under APA because it's explicitly inapplicable here (18 usc 3625)
- The government argues that habeas cannot challenge "level of custody."
- The government argues that statute doesn't allow courts to review BoP place of imprisonment decisions. "[A] designation of a place of imprisonment under this subsection is not reviewable by any court."
- An amicus was appointed to defend the 5th circuit's decision. The amicus argues that the 5th circuit's bright line rule is good. Bright line rules are good and line drawing is bad. "If a prisoner’s claim implies (1) that his physical detention must be shortened or terminated, (2) that he is entitled to earlier release on bail or parole, or (3) that he is being held in a place that the sentencing court did not have jurisdiction to send him, the prisoner’s claim sounds in habeas; otherwise, it does not." (numbers added)

* Younge v Fulton County District Attorney's Office, Gerogia // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/younge-v-fulton-judicial-circuit-district-attorneys-office/
- Younge worked for the DA's office until she was fired in July 2019. She had recently shared that she was pregnant. Younge sued based on discrimination. After discovery the DA's office moved for summary judgement arguing that Younge fell under a "personal-staff exeption" to such claims. Younge argues that the DA can't use that argument because they didn't raise it in their response to her complaint. Affirmative defenses must be raised in response to a complaint, the personal-staff exeption wasn't raised, so it was waived. The district court ruled that the defense could be raised and ruled on the summary judgement in favor of the DA. The 11th circuit affirmed, finding that there was no prejudice to allowing the late-raised defense.

* St. Mary v Roy // https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/10/court-to-hear-argument-in-religious-discrimination-challenge-brought-by-catholic-preschools-in-c/
- Colorado's universal preschool program pays for children's preschooling at approved preschools. Approved preschools must be liscenced, deliver atleast 15 instruction hours per week, comply with disability and IEP requirements, train and liscence special education teachers, offer free tution for eligible families, maintain liability insurance, and comply with the non-discrimination mandate. The nondiscrimination mandate disallows preschools from denying enrollment based on race, ethnicity, religion, lack of housing, income, disabilitiy, sexual orientation, or gender identity. St. Mary requested an exempion allowing them to deny LGBTQ children and children of LGBTQ parents. Colorado denied the exemption. St. Mary, along with the archdiocese and two parent's of preschool aged children sued, arguing that the nondiscrimination requirement violates the first amendment. The district court dismissed the case and the 10th circuit affirmed, finding that the program did not unduly burden religious practice. The nondiscrimination requirement was neutral and not generally applicable. Now St. Mary appeals to SCOTUS.

The court found that because the laws were generally applicable they did not burden the church member's free exercise under rational basis review.
*** Carson v Makin
- Maine created a tuition assistance program. The schools must be nonsectarian and cannot promote a faith or belief system. The court found this violated free exercise under strict scrutiny.
** What does St. Mary have to show to prove lack of "general applicability" under Employment Dvision v Smith?
- Colorado's professed interest is equal opportunity to enroll and receive preschool regardless of protected characteristics. Colorado allows some UPK schools to deny families based on income level. Colorado schools allows some UPK schools to enroll only children with a disability. Odean, director of the UPK program, also testified that UPK schools could admit only gender-nonconforming children, children of color, or lgbtq children. Colorado can't deny UPK access to St. Mary because of discrimination when it allows all these other schools to discriminate. Colorado doesn't get to pick and choose who gets to discriminate and call its law generally applicable.
- Colorado allows UPK schools designed to service disabled children to deny enrollment to children without disabilities. This is necessary to ensure that disabled children have enough space at those schools. If the special needs school enrolls children that could go anywhere then that's fewer seats available for disabled children. Head Start schools similarly provide special instruction for low income children and families. If Head Start schools enroll children that could go anywhere then that's fewer seats available for low income children who require this specialized instruction. Discrimination in these cases are required to pursue Colorado's interest in equal opportunity to enroll and receive preschool.
- Odean was wrong when she testified that schools could admit only gender-nonconforming children, children of color, or lgbtq children. There is no discretion in accepting the equal opportunity requirements. The UKP does not have the power to waive the requirements. The equal opportunity requirements apply to everyone.
** Does Carson v Makin displace Employment Division v Smith only when the government explicitly excludes religious institutions?
- In Carson Maine's tuition assistance program was unavailable to religious schools. Here the UPK system is a tuition assistance program that is unavailable to petitioner's religious schools. Carson displaces Employment Division and triggers strict scrutiny.
- In Carson Maine's tuition assistance explicitly excluded religious education. Employment Division involved neutral, generally applicable laws, which only incidentally burdened religious exercise. Employment Division holds triggering rational basis review.

* Guerrero v Johnson // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/guerrero-v-johnson/
- In 2007 Dexter Johnson was convicted of double murder and sentenced to death. Under Atkins v Virginia it violates the 8th amendment to sentence a mentally retarded defendant to death. Johnson's iq measured between 74 and 88, which wasn't low enough to be mentally considered mentally retarded. Johnson appealed anyway arguing that Atkins should be extended to the mentally ill. In 2013 while his appeal was pending the Texas criminal court adopted the updated DSM which found that someone with an iq over 70 can still be intellectually disabled (we don't call it mentally retarded anymore). He filed a successive habeas petition arguing that he is intellectually disabled and so can't be executed on Atkins. Under AEDPA you may not file a successive habeas petition unless you can show that the claim relies on a "new rule of constitutional law" that was "previously unavailable". The district court found that Johnson's Atkins claim qualified and stayed his execution. The 5th circuit affirmed, noting a circuit split. Now the State (Guerrero is the Director of the Texas Deptarment of Criminal Justice, Correctional Institutions Division.)
** Does Johnson's Atkins claim qualify for the successive habeas exception?
- The State argues that Johnson's Atkins claim does not rely on a change in law. Atkins was decided before Johnson's crime. Atkins was always available to him. Johnson's claim relies on new facts found in the DSM-5. Not a rule of constitutional law.
- Johnson argues that Atkins was "previously unavailable." Johnson could not make an Atkins claim because he did not meet the standard for intellectual disability. The standards changed. Atkins is now available to Johnson. Johnson should get his chance to use the "new rule" in Atkins.

* Nielson v Watanabe // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/nielsen-v-watanabe/
- Watanabe was serving 57 months in prison for having ammunition as a felon. While in prison he was attacked in a gang riot. His attackers fractured his coccyx, lodging bone fragments in the surrounding soft tissue. Francis Nielson was the nurse a the prison. Nielson gave him over-the-counter pain medication, told him to "stop being a cry baby", and refused to have him treated at the hospital. Watanabe spent the next two months in solitary confinement where he continued to complain of pain. Months later Watanabe as diagnosed with a fractured coccyx and transfered to a specialist. Watanabe sued Nielson and other BOP officials under Bivens. The district court found that Watanabe had an administrative remedy through the BOP's Administrative Remedy Program. That distinction made this a new Bivens context and counciled against extending Bivens. The 9th circuit reversed, finding that Watanabe's claim was the same as the one in Carlson v Green. So this is not a new Bivens context so Watanabe's suit can proceed. Now Nielson appeals to SCOTUS.
*** Bivens v Six Unknown Federal Narcotics Agents
- The Federal Beureu of Narcotics raided Biven's without a warrant and arrested him on narcotics charges. Bivens sued the agents for violating his 4th amendment rights. SCOTUS recognized a right of action to sue when federal agents violate your 4th amendment.
*** Carlson v Green
- SCOTUS extended Bivens to a case where a federal prison failed to render aid to an inmate, leading to his death.
** Did the 9th circuit err in recognizing a Biven's cause of action?
- Bivens claims must survive a two part test. 1: Does the claim arise in a new context (in this case different from Carlson)? 2: Are there special factors counciling hesitation to find a cause of action?
- Nielson argues that Watanabe's claim fails part 1. Carlson died shortly after he was attacked in prison. Watanabe's injuries were never life threatening. Carlson could only seek compesatory damages. Watanabe can a change in conditions or additional treatment.
- Nielson argues that Watanabe's claim fails part 2. Congress created the Administrative Remedy Program. Prisoner greivences belong there, not court.
- Nielson argues that Bivens should be overturned. It's the power and duty of congress to create private rights of action.
- The government doesn't support overturning Bivens here but does want to confine Carlson to its facts. The ARP and PLRA have moved the law.
- Watanabe argues that his claim succeeds at part 1. The controlling fact of Carlson was: prison officers were deliberately indifferent to his needs in violation of the 8th amendment. Watanabe's prison officers were deliberately indifferent to his medical needs in violation of the 8th amendment.
- Watanabe argues that Bivens was not egregeously wrong and that congress has reliance interests in the holding. Overtunrning Bivens would leave prisoners with no recourse to 8th amendment violations, a situation Congress never legislated for.

* Hoffman v WBI Energy Transmission // https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/hoffmann-v-wbi-energy-transmission-inc/
- WBI Energy Transmission stores and transports natural gas. When WBI builds a new pipeline is applies to the Federal Energy Regulatory Comission for a certificate of public convinience and necessity (CPCN). Then WBI buys the land, or an easement, for the pipeline. Hoffman declined to sell their land. WBI brought a federal condemnation action, exercising eminent domain power through its CPCN. They eventually agreed to a just compensation. Under North Dakota law the government must pay the target's attorney's fees after exercising emminent domain. Applying that law here the district court made WBI pay the agreed compensation plus Hoffman's attorney's fees. WBI appealed to the 8th circuit which found that federal law applied and federal law does not required the government to pay attoreny's fees. Now Hoffman appeals to SCOTUS, incuring more attorney's fees.
** Does state law control just compensation in private condemnation actions under the Natural Gas Act?
- The Hoffmanns argue that State law controls. When federal law is silent state law controls. The Natural Gas Act is silence on which law controls. Therefore state law controls.
- WBI argues that Federal law grants WBI emminent domain so federal law controls. WBI is exercising that federal governments eminent domain power, not the states.
- WBI also points to the General Bridge Act (which is not the Natural Gas Act, but was adopted around the same time). The General Bridge Act states that just compensation must "be ascertained and paid according to the laws of [the] State". The Natrual Gas Act doesn't say that, so just comensation for land taken under the Natural Gas Act must not "be ascertained and paid according to the laws of [the] state."
